Zoning is what makes ducted air conditioning economical. Instead of heating or cooling the whole house every time you switch it on, you choose which areas get air — living areas during the day, bedrooms at night.

Get the zones right and a ducted system can be surprisingly cheap to run. Get them wrong and you'll be cooling empty bedrooms all afternoon.

## How ducted zoning works

Every ducted system has one indoor unit in the roof pushing air through a network of ducts. **Zoning adds motorised dampers** to those ducts. When a zone is switched off, its damper closes and air only goes to the zones that are on.

Each zone can be a single room or a group of rooms that are used together. You control the zones from the wall controller — or from your phone with a smart controller.

## How many zones do you need?

A good starting point is to **group rooms by when you use them**:

- **Living zone:** kitchen, family, dining — used during the day and evening
- **Master bedroom:** often its own zone, especially if it has an ensuite or walk-in
- **Kids' bedrooms:** one zone or split in two
- **Theatre / study / activity room:** its own zone if it's used separately

As a rough guide for single-storey Perth homes:

| Home | Typical zones |
|---|---|
| 2–3 bedroom | 3–4 |
| 4 bedroom | 4–6 |
| 5+ bedroom | 6–8 |

These aren't rules — a 4 bedroom home where the family lives in one big open-plan area might be perfectly happy with four zones, while one with a separate theatre, study and kids' retreat might want seven.

## Standard controller vs smart zone control

Every ducted system comes with a wall controller that can turn zones on and off. A **smart zone controller** goes further:

- **Individual room temperatures** — each zone can hold its own temperature using sensors
- **Schedules** — bedrooms on at 9pm, living areas off overnight
- **App control** — switch it on from your phone on the way home
- **Smart-home integration** — link it with other smart home devices you already have

## Common zoning mistakes

1. **Too few zones.** Grouping bedrooms with living areas means cooling bedrooms all day. It's cheap to split them during the install.
2. **Too many tiny zones.** A ducted system needs somewhere for the air to go. If only one small room is open, the system has to bleed air elsewhere (through a "spill" zone) or it runs inefficiently.
3. **Ignoring how you use the house.** Zones should match your routine, not just the floor plan.
4. **No return air planning.** Closed bedroom doors and a single central return grille can starve rooms of airflow. Placement matters.

## Does zoning really save money?

Yes. If a ducted system is only conditioning two of five zones, it's doing a fraction of the work. This is why a well-zoned ducted system is often comparable to running a few split systems — see our [ducted vs split system comparison](/blog/ducted-vs-split-system).
